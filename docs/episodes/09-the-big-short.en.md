# The Big <span class="ms-outline">Short.</span>

## Why this episode is the synthesis of the whole series

This trade combines everything we have learned since episode 6. The CDS (paying a premium to bet on a credit deterioration), the correlation between underlying assets (CDO note), and the hidden fragility behind an AAA/BBB rating. It is Michael Burry's trade, the one told in *The Big Short*.

## The context, 2005

Bonds backed by subprime loans (MBS) were sliced into CDO tranches and rated investment grade by the agencies. Burry did not trust the ratings. He went through the pool data himself, loan by loan.

## What he found by digging into the pool data

**1. Time-bomb ARMs.** Most of these loans were ARMs (Adjustable Rate Mortgages) of the "2/28" or "3/27" type, a low fixed rate (teaser rate) for 2-3 years, then a sharp reset upward. A huge number of loans were going to reset at roughly the same time (2005 vintage around 2007, 2006 vintage around 2008).

**2. Collapsing underwriting standards.** More and more loans with little or no income verification (the "liar loans" / NINJA loans, No Income No Job no Assets) and very high loan-to-value ratios (almost no down payment).

## The hidden assumption everything depended on

A fragile borrower who could no longer pay once their rate reset had two ways out, refinance the loan or sell the house at a gain. Both options **only work if house prices keep rising**. So it was not 1000 independent, diversified loans. It was **a single shared risk factor** (continuously rising prices) disguised as diversification, exactly Scenario B from the CDO note.

## The trigger, no crash needed

Burry did not need to predict a violent collapse. All he needed was for prices to **simply stop rising**. As soon as that happens, refinancing or selling at a profit becomes impossible for fragile borrowers, **at the exact moment** their rates reset. That turns defaults that should have trickled in one by one into a **simultaneous wave**.

## The actual trade

He specifically targeted the **BBB-rated mezzanine tranches** of subprime MBS, not the equity (already seen as risky, so expensive to short). The market treated BBB mezzanine as reasonably safe, so the cost of protection (the CDS) was **abnormally cheap** relative to the real risk it carried. He bought that protection through CDS (reminder, paying a regular premium while betting on a deterioration).

## The outcome

When house prices stalled in 2007, correlated defaults cut through entire tranches, some of them completely wiped out (write-down close to 100%, recovery close to 0%). His CDS paid out on losses the market had rated as unlikely.

## Key takeaway

The lesson is not really about real estate. A rating built on a diversification assumption is only worth as much as that assumption. When the assumption breaks (here, continuously rising prices), the whole rating collapses with it.
