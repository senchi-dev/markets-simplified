# The Yield <span class="ms-outline">Curve.</span>

## Definition

The **yield curve** shows, at a given point in time, the **yield** of bonds with the **same credit quality** (often government bonds, e.g. US Treasuries or French OATs) but **different maturities**, 3 months, 1 year, 2 years, 5 years, 10 years, 30 years...

Maturity is on the x-axis. Yield is on the y-axis. Connect the points and you get a curve.

## Why it exists (the basic intuition)

Normally, lending money for longer should pay more, because you take on more risk over a longer period (inflation risk, rate risk, economic uncertainty). That is why the expected "default" shape is **upward sloping**.

## The 3 shapes of the curve

### 1. Normal / upward sloping

Long rates are higher than short rates. This is the most common and the "healthiest" shape. The market expects normal economic growth, with rates likely to rise or stay stable, a period of **expansion**.

### 2. Inverted

Long rates are lower than short rates. Counterintuitive, lending for 10 years pays **less** than lending for 3 months.

It happens because investors expect the central bank to **cut** rates in the future, since they foresee a slowdown or a recession. They rush into **long** bonds to lock in the current rate before it falls. This creates strong demand and pushes **prices** up, and since price and yield move in **opposite directions**, **long yields fall** mechanically.

Historically it is one of the best predictors of **recession** (the 2Y/10Y inversion has preceded almost every US recession over the past 50 years, with a lag of 6 to 24 months).

### 3. Flat

Little difference between short and long rates. The market is torn between a growth scenario and a slowdown scenario, which reflects high **uncertainty**. It is often a transition phase between a normal curve and an inversion (or the reverse).

## Why price and yield move in opposite directions (key reminder)

A bond pays fixed cash flows (coupons + principal). If the bond's market price **falls**, the same fixed cash flow represents a **higher return** relative to the price paid, so the yield **rises**. Conversely, if the price **rises**, the yield **falls**. It is mechanical, not a coincidence.

## Theories that explain the shape of the curve (going further)

- **Expectations theory.** The curve purely reflects the market's expectations of future short rates. If the market expects rate hikes, the curve slopes upward.
- **Liquidity preference theory.** Investors demand an extra premium (liquidity premium) to agree to lock up their money for longer, which naturally pushes the curve upward, even without expected hikes.
- **Market segmentation theory.** Different investors (banks, insurers, pension funds) have different maturity needs and do not arbitrage perfectly between segments, which can create local distortions in the curve.

## Why it is a real decision-making tool

- A portfolio manager who sees the curve invert can **reduce exposure to cyclical equities** and move into defensive assets.
- Central banks watch the curve to calibrate monetary policy.
- Traders bet on the **shape** of the curve itself, not just its level. A "steepener" bets that the long-short gap will widen, a "flattener" bets the opposite.

## Classic pitfalls to avoid

- Confusing the **level** of rates (all rates rise or fall together) with the **shape** of the curve (the gap between short and long rates changes). These are two different things.
- An inverted curve does not tell you *when* exactly the recession will arrive, only that the market expects one.

## Key takeaway

The yield curve encodes the market's **collective expectations** about the future path of rates and the economy. Its **inversion** is one of the most closely watched macro signals in the world.
