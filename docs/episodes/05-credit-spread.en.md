# Credit <span class="ms-outline">Spread.</span>

## Definition

The **credit spread** is the **extra** yield an investor demands to lend to a **risky** borrower (a company) rather than to a borrower considered **risk-free** (a solid government, in its own currency).

**Formula**

`Credit spread = Corporate bond yield − Government bond yield (same maturity)`

**Worked example.** A 10-year corporate bond yields 5.5%. The 10-year government bond (the "risk-free" benchmark) yields 3.2%. The credit spread is 5.5% − 3.2% = **2.3%, or 230 bps.**

## Why you always compare at the same maturity

A bond's yield depends both on **credit risk** and on the **general level of rates** (seen in the Yield Curve). By comparing two bonds of the **same maturity**, you isolate the effect of credit risk alone. Otherwise you would be comparing apples and oranges, and part of the gap would just come from the shape of the yield curve.

## What a credit spread is really made of (the subtle point)

The spread is **not** a pure reflection of default risk. It combines several components.

1. **Default risk premium.** The probability that the company does not repay, weighted by the potential loss.
2. **Liquidity premium.** Corporate bonds trade less easily than government bonds, so investors demand compensation for that lack of liquidity.
3. **Uncertainty / risk aversion premium.** Even with no change in the actual probability of default, if investors become more nervous overall (a period of macro stress), they demand a wider spread everywhere, on every company.
4. Sometimes a **tax** component (different tax treatment of government vs corporate bonds depending on the jurisdiction).

That is why spreads can widen even if a specific company's default risk has not changed. The market is repricing liquidity or general uncertainty, not just that company's own credit.

## Why spreads are watched so closely

The credit spread is a **double signal**.

- It reflects the issuer's **perceived credit quality**.
- It reflects **overall market confidence**. In periods of stress (2008, March 2020), spreads widen sharply across **the whole market**, even for solid companies, because the liquidity and uncertainty premiums explode.

In practice, spreads are a macro barometer watched almost as closely as equity indices.

## Example of widening during a crisis

In 2008, IG corporate credit spreads went from about 100-150 bps to more than **600 bps** in a few months. Not because all these companies suddenly became 6 times more likely to go bankrupt, but because liquidity dried up and panic drove the uncertainty premium up everywhere.

<div class="ms-takeaway" markdown>

## Key takeaway

The credit spread measures the price of credit risk, but it is a **composite** signal, a mix of credit quality, liquidity and market confidence. Never read it as a pure measure of default risk.
